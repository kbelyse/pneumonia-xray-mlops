These numbers come from all 5,856 original images (before resizing). I looked at them before training to understand what the model might pick up on, including things it should not.

1. Class balance

Almost three out of four training images are pneumonia. A model that always says pneumonia would already get 74% accuracy on train and 62.5% on test, so accuracy alone says little here. That is why I used class weights and also report recall, specificity and F1. The test set is more balanced than train, which made my first models look worse on test than on validation.

2. Brightness in the centre of the image

The centre of a pneumonia X-ray is brighter (median 152 vs 139 out of 255). On an X-ray, air looks black and fluid looks white, so infected lungs filled with fluid show up as white patches. Viral cases were the brightest (155), bacterial 151. The whole-image brightness is the same for both classes (about 122), so the difference really sits in the lung area and not in the exposure of the image.

3. Contrast

Pneumonia images have lower contrast (pixel standard deviation 55 vs 61). In a healthy chest the dark lungs stand out sharply against the white ribs and heart. When the lungs fill with fluid that edge gets washed out and the image looks hazy. I added random contrast changes during training so the model does not lean only on this.

4. Original image size

This one worried me. Normal images are much bigger (median 1654 x 1323 pixels) than pneumonia images (1160 x 776), and their shape is different too (width/height 1.24 vs 1.49). Size has nothing to do with the disease, it probably comes from different machines or cropping. A model could learn "small image means pneumonia". Resizing every image to 224 x 224 removes the size, but the stretching is still different per class, so this is a real limitation of the dataset.

5. Average image per class

The average normal chest has two clear dark lung shapes. In the pneumonia average they are blurry and lighter, so the fluid is spread over the lungs and not in one fixed place. The difference map is red over both lungs, strongest in the middle and lower parts. It is blue at the top and bottom, meaning the normal images show more of the neck and belly. That fits the different shape I found in the size chart.

One more thing I only noticed here: there is a small letter R on the left side of the average normal image. Many normal X-rays have the side marker in exactly the same spot, while in the pneumonia images it moves around and blurs away. Like the image size, that has nothing to do with the lungs, and it is the kind of shortcut I would check first if I had more time (for example by covering the corners before training).
